# Do in-silico metrics predict experimental binding?

A retrospective calibration study on the published Anthropic de novo binder
campaign. Generated by `binderkit study retrospective`; every number here
comes from that script and is reproducible from it.

## Bottom line

On 1320 designs against 15 targets (354 measured binders, 26.8%), the best single in-silico metric is **`ipsae_mean`**.

- **Pooled AUROC 0.764** (95% CI 0.671-0.839, bootstrap resampling targets)
- **Mean within-target AUROC 0.761** across the 14 targets where both outcomes occur

The best trivial baseline, `hydrophobic_fraction`, reaches 0.589 within-target, so the real headroom over a non-predictor is **+0.172**.

### Averaging the predictors helps; learning weights over them does not

The best *individual* predictor is `ipsae_min_ptxv2` at 0.733 within-target. The plain mean across all ten predictors reaches 0.761, a lift of **+0.028**. Cross-predictor consensus is worth more than any one predictor, which is an argument for running several cheap folds over one expensive one.

A leave-one-target-out logistic regression over all 27 metric columns reaches within-target **0.726** (pooled 0.701, 95% CI 0.634-0.767).

**That is -0.034 against the simple unweighted mean, so the learned model is worse than the average it was given.** Fitting weights on fourteen targets and applying them to a fifteenth overfits: the relative reliability of the predictors does not transfer across targets, while their average does. The practical reading is to average the predictors and not to learn weights over them on a dataset of this size.

### Pooled AUROC is biased, but not always upward

Pooling designs from every target can credit a metric for separating easy targets from hard ones rather than ranking designs. The size and the **direction** of that bias turn out to depend on the metric family:

| Family | median pooled - within-target gap |
|---|---|
| cross-predictor consensus | +0.015 |
| cross-predictor disagreement | -0.037 |
| single-predictor DockQ | +0.066 |
| single-predictor ipSAE | +0.017 |
| trivial baseline | -0.039 |

The most inflated single metric is `sc_dockq_ef2full` (single-predictor DockQ), which looks +0.084 better pooled than it is within a target. The most deflated is `mean_hydropathy` (trivial baseline), which is 0.057 *better* within target than pooled AUROC implies. So the correction is not a constant that can be subtracted off: it has to be computed per metric.

For the headline metric the two agree to within 0.003, which is the reassuring case: `ipsae_mean` is genuinely ranking designs inside a target, not just ranking targets. That was worth checking rather than assuming, and the assumption would have been wrong for DockQ.

### Predictor disagreement is informative, in the inverse direction

`ipsae_std_across` scores 0.370 within-target, well **below** 0.5. An AUROC below chance is not noise: it means the metric is predictive with its sign flipped. Inverted it would score about 0.630. Designs the ten predictors agree about are more likely to bind, and spread across predictors is a usable negative signal in its own right.

### The ceiling on any metric

The two CROs disagree with each other. Of 1,235 designs measured by both,
253 bind at both, 846 at neither, 69 at Adaptyv only and 67 at Twist only:
**89.0% agreement**. No in-silico metric should be expected to predict the
adjudicated label better than two wet-lab assays agree, so about 0.89 is a
soft ceiling on achievable accuracy here, not 1.0.

## Recommended operating point

On `ipsae_mean`, maximising the Youden J statistic:

- threshold **0.635**
- sensitivity 0.743, specificity 0.684
- precision 0.463, recall 0.743
- selects 568 of 1320 designs

At this threshold about 46% of selected designs were measured binders, against a base rate of 26.8%. That is a real improvement, and it still means most selected designs do not bind.

## Conclusions

1. **The metrics carry real signal.** `ipsae_mean` reaches 0.761 within-target AUROC against 0.589 for the best trivial baseline, and the effect survives grouping by target in both the folds and the bootstrap.
2. **Average the predictors; do not learn weights over them.** The unweighted mean of ten predictors beat every individual predictor, and a leave-one-target-out logistic regression over all 27 metric columns did worse than that mean. On fifteen targets, relative predictor reliability does not transfer; the average does.
3. **Check pooled against within-target AUROC per metric, because the bias is signed.** DockQ metrics look up to 0.08 better pooled than they are within a target; ipSAE metrics mostly look slightly worse. Reporting only pooled AUROC would have overstated one family and understated the other.
4. **No metric is safe as a universal hard filter.** The best metric is at or below chance on 1 of 14 targets, so a fixed cut-off would discard good designs on those targets.
5. **Enrichment is real and modest.** At the recommended threshold, 46% of selected designs were binders against a 26.8% base rate: roughly 1.7x enrichment, and still a majority of selected designs that do not bind.
6. **The wet lab agrees with itself only 89% of the time**, which bounds what any in-silico metric can be asked to achieve.

## Limitations

- One campaign, two CROs, 16 targets, miniproteins of 50-120 residues only.
- These designs were not produced by this repo pipeline, so the thresholds
  transfer only as far as the metric definitions match.
- `binder_final` is an adjudicated label over two disagreeing assays, not a
  direct measurement. Per-vendor calls would give a slightly different answer.
- Several K_D values in the release are apparent rather than true (oligomeric
  analytes, differing antigen forms), so this study uses the binary call only.
- Logistic regression with median imputation and no tuning. A stronger model
  might extract more, though the within-target ceiling suggests the headroom
  is small.
